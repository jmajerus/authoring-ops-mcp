Gabrielle Collet’s Zero Spaces names something real about how art gets made, then never leaves it

The Space Before Judgment

Gabrielle Collet’s Zero Spaces names something real about how art gets made, then never leaves it

Zero Spaces: Poïesis and the Art of Collaborative Creativity, by Gabrielle Collet. Foreword by Alessandro De Francesco. Atropos Press, Think Media: EGS Media Philosophy Series. 243 pages.

Anyone who makes things knows the moment. The work stops being something you push and becomes something you receive. Plato credited the Muses. Schiller, in a letter Collet uses as her first epigraph, said the intellect withdraws its watchers from the gates. Gabrielle Collet calls it a zero space.

In her words, this “non-tangible, indescribable, timeless space where the so-called transcendent/immanent occurs within an individual, stimulating the apparition of creation, came to be conceptually known as, (for lack of a better nomination), a zero space.” (Collet 2014, 16)1 The word zero reaches English through the Arabic sifr, “empty,” and Collet likes that its symbol is also a circle. The question she sets herself is practical. If this state is as universal as the historical record suggests, what would happen if artists entered it together, on purpose?

That is a good question, and an unusual one for a book of theory. Zero Spaces began as a doctoral thesis at the European Graduate School and keeps the shape of one: three parts, more than two hundred footnotes, a long bibliography. But Collet says plainly in her preface that she simplified her concepts and her language on purpose, for poets with little patience for theory. That candor is the first of the book’s virtues. Many of its problems trace back to the same choice.

What is in it

Part I, “The Theories,” works through three words. Zero gets a tour of quantum zero-point energy, religious circles from the Kabbalah to the Tibetan wheel of life, and Zeno’s dichotomy paradox. Poiesis gets Plato’s Symposium, Vico, Heidegger, and Bergson. Genius gets its Greek and Roman genealogy, largely by way of Darrin McMahon’s history of the idea. Part II, “The Arts,” offers three “synoptic archeologies,” of poetry, visual art, and sound, each running from the caves to the present. Part III proposes an institution: an annual gathering of poets in four disciplines (poetry, visual art, sound, performance) who would produce what Collet calls “Fictive World Formulae,” shared myths for a fractured age, broadcast free to everyone.

Collet knows the scope invites trouble. She concedes that “to try and deal in one work with a range of texts, art, music and performance spanning the period from cavemen to the contemporary world would be to invite derision, if not ridicule. No matter.” (Collet 2014, 17)

The pleasures

The book is best when it plays. Its most memorable chapter is a one-act play. Derrida sits at his desk reading from “Cogito and the History of Madness” while the history of Western letters barges in. Byron struts on with a cane. Husserl emerges, coughing, from a closet. Gide flutters down on cupid wings in a cloth diaper; Nietzsche appears in a Superman costume with a Z on his chest. Milton and Coleridge phone in. At one point a wall-sized iPad flashes a Huffington Post quotation from a creativity researcher, and Derrida answers with mock gratitude:

“Astonishing how as modern technology advances, language used within it becomes increasingly more vapid” (Collet 2014, 45).

The device, thinkers from different centuries talking past one another in one room, earns the comparison the foreword draws with Plato’s dialogues. Elsewhere the jokes are smaller.

After several pages on Bergson’s duration, Collet asks, “Can you imagine watching Heidegger dance?” (Collet 2014, 82). The sound chapter ends with a heading, “7.8 Silence,” over a blank page, a nod to John Cage’s 4′33″, which she discusses a few pages before.

The diagnosis that opens the book also deserves attention. Collet argues that the fact that, “with the simplistic motion of our finger, we have access to an immeasurable world of visual(s), objects, musical compositions, compilations of sound bites, and literary texts which traverse through centuries of creations until the contemporary day, has crippled our need for new art.” (Collet 2014, 23) Drawing on John Hope Mason’s The Value of Creativity, she pairs this with the loss of shared myth: more “creatives,” less significance. Readers who worry about what unlimited access does to making will recognize the problem.

The trouble with no thinking

The difficulty begins with the concept itself. Collet insists that the zero space comes before thought.

For when one enters a zero space, there is simply no thinking what-so-ever. (Collet 2014, 76)

It is a state of suspended judgment, to be followed, she says, by a return to reason in which the maker shapes what was received.

The book performs the first half of that cycle and skips the second. Its method is association. Wikipedia is cited for the history of zero, for musical dissonance, and for the theologian who coined Gesamtkunstwerk. Zero-point energy is summarized without attribution and turned into a meditation on breathing. A university web outline does much of the work on the Symposium. Sources are collected rather than weighed.

The results are sometimes loose and sometimes plainly wrong. Of the Epic of Gilgamesh and the Egyptian Tale of the Shipwrecked Sailor, Collet writes that “both are said to date back to around 4500 B.C.E.” (Collet 2014, 102). That is more than two thousand years too early for either.

Dante appears as “having become literally blind” (Collet 2014, 113), which he did not. Joseph Beuys is credited with founding the Conceptual Art movement. A timeline of sound art dates every entry, from Alvin Lucier to Lou Reed’s Metal Machine Music, to the first of January, which suggests the dates were copied from a source that recorded only years. An epigraph about Resnais, Prigogine, and Godard is attributed to Alan Sokal.2

Some lapses are worse than careless. Collet sets Plato beside Hitler in a subordinate clause and moves on: “Hence, eradicating the absolute authority which Plato, not unlike Hitler, sought.” (Collet 2014, 59) She speculates that Heidegger might have benefited from a dose of MDMA, citing a website about the drug.

She returns more than once to artists who did not come back from the zero space, as though suicide were an occupational hazard of insight. Her account of David Foster Wallace ends with “suicide as sole liberation” (Collet 2014, 155). A book that argues for collaboration as a way to keep artists from being lost owed that subject more care.

Where collaboration finally appears

The title promises a theory of collaborative creativity. For most of the book, collaboration is an aspiration rather than a subject. It arrives in earnest near the end of the visual-arts survey, where Collet takes up the actual literature on collaborative and participatory art: Grant Kester’s The One and the Many, Claire Bishop’s Artificial Hells, and Jacques Rancière’s The Emancipated Spectator. These are the book’s most substantive pages.

They also contain its most revealing miss. Collet quotes Bishop’s portrait of one camp of participatory art, the camp for which “What counts is to offer ameliorative solutions, however short term, rather than the exposure of contradictory social truths.”3

Then she sides with Bishop’s doubts about art that dissolves into social process: “She has a point: if there are no visuals, how exactly can we call it visual art?” (Collet 2014, 207).

Yet the project in her conclusion fits the ameliorative camp almost exactly. It calls for perhaps thirty poets a year, a foundation funded by tax-deductible donations, and a rotation through every country on the map, so that “the project proposed would essentially be complete every seven years” (Collet 2014, 240). Its stated aim is to wake humanity up. Collet does not notice the tension.

Two better maps

Two books in my library approach Collet’s question from other directions, and both manage what hers does not.

John Paul Lederach’s The Moral Imagination is about peacebuilders, not poets. But it treats suspended judgment as a discipline rather than an absence: “To suspend judgment is not to relinquish opinion or the capacity to assess” (Lederach 2005, chap. 4).4 For Lederach, the point of withholding judgment is to judge better, to see a conflict whole before deciding what to do in it.

He insists, too, that “The moral imagination takes form and expression through an act” (Lederach 2005, chap. 4). His examples are local and relational: a young man who treats an enemy chief as a father, market women who make a town safe. Collet’s zero space leaves no room for assessment, and her remedy works at the scale of the planet.

Hannah Arendt described the shape of that remedy long ago. In The Human Condition she named “the Platonic wish to substitute making for acting” (Floridi 2015, 171): the hope that the unpredictable business of people acting together could be replaced by a craftsman’s work, planned and finished.5 Fictive World Formulae are a version of that wish. A global ecological and political crisis is to be met with a better-made myth, distributed through the same screens Collet blames for crippling our need for art. The problem of acting together becomes a problem of making something together.

The foreword, by the poet Alessandro De Francesco, sums up the thesis generously: “collaboration consists in generating possibility” (Collet 2014, 10). Possibility is real, but so is the step after it.

The book closes its own circle in a way that makes that step harder to see. De Francesco is thanked in the acknowledgments as the person without whom Collet might not have finished. He receives the final section of her survey of poetry. In the conclusion he is the model of the artist the project hopes to produce: “Alessandro de Francesco, in his work, and in his Being, found that balance” (Collet 2014, 241). Whatever the merits of his work, a theory of collaboration whose chief exhibit is its own foreword’s author has not yet faced an outside reader.

Verdict

Zero Spaces names something worth naming. Its central wish, that artists in different media might enter that silence together and bring back something a fragmented public could share, is generous. Its dialogues are inventive, and its pages on Bishop and Kester ask the right question. But the book mistakes accumulation for argument, and it rarely tests a source before using it.

By Collet’s own account, the zero space has to be followed by a return to reason: the editing, the weighing, the judgment that turns what was received into something made. This book stays on the near side of that return. Poets looking for permission to take their own blank spaces seriously may find it here. Readers looking for a reliable map of the territory will need to check every landmark.

This review concentrates on Parts I and III, the one-act play, and the chapters on collaborative art and sound. The long surveys of poetry and visual art were sampled rather than read in full, so their readings of individual poets and painters go unassessed here.
